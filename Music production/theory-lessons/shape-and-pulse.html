Shape & Pulse

Four short lessons that treat harmony as shapes on a wheel and blocks in a piano roll, and rhythm as pulses on a grid. Every diagram plays. Click things, hear them, then take the idea to Live, VCV or the Hapax.

LESSON 1 · about 10 minutes

A chord is a shape you can move

The 12 notes sit on a clock face. A chord is a set of points on that clock, joined into a shape. Every major chord has the same shape, every minor chord has the same shape, and moving to another key just rotates it.

  1. Stack two gaps. Pick Minor. Its shape is a gap of 3 steps, then 4 (♭3 then 5). Major is the same two gaps the other way round: 4 then 3.
  2. Rotate it. Change the root. The triangle spins round the clock but never changes shape. That is transposition.
  3. Keep stacking. Try Min7, Min9, Min11. Each one adds another gap of 3 or 4. The piano roll shows the stack growing upwards: this is the lush minor-9 and minor-11 stab colour that runs through Detroit pads.
  4. Free play. Click points on the wheel to toggle notes. The name updates if the shape is a known chord.

Switch the wheel to fifths order: chords that sound stable bunch together into tight clusters, and tense chords spread out.

Take it to the rig

Hapax: open the chord playground, pick any chord, and say its gaps out loud (for example 3-4-3 for a min7). If you know the gaps you can build it in any key without the screen. VCV: feed a poly cable into a scope or the Changes module and watch the voltages step by 1/12 V per semitone: a gap of 3 is 0.25 V. Exercise: make a 4-bar loop that only uses one shape (say min9) rotated to four roots.

LESSON 2 · about 12 minutes

Borrow one chord from a neighbouring mode

The modes on one root form a ladder from bright to dark. Each step down the ladder lowers exactly one note. Modal interchange means staying on your home mode but borrowing a chord from another rung for a moment.

  1. Read the ladder. Your home mode is marked. Click another rung to pick where you borrow from. The blue cells are the notes that differ.
  2. Spot the borrowed chords. In the chord row underneath, chords with a blue underline don't exist in your home mode. Faded ones are the same as at home.
  3. Swap one slot. The loop starts as Cm, A♭, Fm, B♭. Play it, then click Dorian's IV (F major) to put it in slot 3. One note changes (A♭ becomes A) and the whole loop lifts.
  4. Go darker. Try the Phrygian preset: the ♭II chord, a semitone above home, gives that menacing pull.

Roman numerals are written against the major scale, so ♭VI means "the chord built a minor 6th above home".

Take it to the rig

Modular: run Marbles or Metropolix through a quantizer set to minor, then switch it to Dorian for one bar in eight. That is modal interchange applied to a melody: one note changes, everything else holds. Live: duplicate a chord clip, change only the borrowed chord, and follow-action between the two clips. Hapax: keep the scale on Aeolian and enter the borrowed chord by hand, so it stands out.

LESSON 3 · about 10 minutes

Change tempo by re-counting a pulse you can already hear

Metric modulation picks one note value in the old tempo, then declares that it is now a different note value in the new tempo. The pulse itself never changes speed at the switch. Your ear simply starts counting it differently, so the jump sounds inevitable rather than like a gear change.

  1. Watch the middle lane. The thread of pivot dots keeps exactly the same spacing across the switch. Only the beats around it change.
  2. Play the jungle preset. At 128, a dotted 8th lasts the same time as a quarter at 170.7. The dotted 8th you hear in bar 2 becomes the kick in bar 3.
  3. Turn on Ghost. The old 128 keeps ticking quietly underneath, so you can hear both grids lock at the bar line.
  4. Build your own. Change OLD and NEW. The new tempo is the old tempo times NEW ÷ OLD (in beats).

This lesson is the theory behind your Pivot module: each Pivot step is one of these OLD = NEW equations, and its GHOST output is the ghost lane here.

Arrangement idea: this is a way out of loop fatigue: the groove changes completely while one element carries straight through.

LESSON 4 · about 10 minutes

Swap the V chord for the one directly opposite it on the wheel

A dominant 7th chord gets its pull from two notes six steps apart: a tritone. On the clock face, six steps is exactly half way round, so those two notes sit at opposite ends of a line through the centre. Spin the whole chord half way round and that line lands back on the same two notes. The new chord does the same job with a different bass note. That's tritone substitution.

  1. Find the line. In G7 the tritone is B and F, the 3 and ♭7. The thick line through the middle of the wheel joins them.
  2. Spin it half way. Switch to Substitute. G7 becomes D♭7, the dashed shape opposite. Its 3 and ♭7 are F and C♭ (which is B): the same line, flipped.
  3. Hear the bass slide. Play the ii–V–I both ways. The original bass jumps D, G, C. With the sub it creeps down by semitones: D, D♭, C.
  4. Try it in minor. The Minor setting lands on Cm9, closer to the Detroit pad sound. The D♭7 now also casts a dark Phrygian shadow, like Lesson 2's ♭II.

The rule in one line: any dominant 7th can be replaced by the dominant 7th 6 semitones away, because they share their 3 and ♭7.

Take it to the rig

VCV or modular: on the bar with the V chord, add 0.5 V to the chord's root CV (six semitones is half an octave). Patch it from a sequencer lane or a DROID switch so you can flip the substitute in and out live. Hapax: copy the V chord and transpose it by 6. Live: on the V chord's MIDI clip, select only the bass note and move it 6 semitones. Even with the upper notes unchanged you hear most of the effect, because the bass is the part that moves.

THE WHOLE COURSE

Three ways the course could be delivered

Recommended

Web lessons + a rig take-away

Pages like this one: 10-minute lessons, each with one playable diagram and one exercise. Every lesson ends with a small VCV patch or Live set that puts the same idea on your own gear.

  • Works on phone and laptop
  • Fastest to make, so the course grows quickly
  • The rig step stops it staying abstract
Option

Max for Live devices

The diagrams become MIDI devices in Live: a chord wheel that outputs chords, an interchange device that bends incoming notes to a borrowed mode, a tempo-pivot device.

  • Learning happens inside a track you're writing
  • Doubles as tools for the EP
  • Slower to build per lesson, explanations live in a PDF
Option

VCV lesson patches

Each lesson is a .vcv patch with notes on the panel, using modules you already have (Pivot, The Changes, quantizers). You learn by turning knobs and re-patching.

  • Closest to how you already work
  • Great for rhythm and sequencing ideas
  • Harder for harmony, where you need to see notes
