1 · THE COMPOSER

Steve Reich: pulse, pattern and process
Reich (born New York, 3 October 1936) is the best known of the composers who walked away from postwar modernism and rebuilt music around a steady pulse, clear harmony and repetition. His central idea is
that the process that generates a piece should be audible: you hear the machine working, and the machine
is the music.

From tape loops to ensembles
He studied philosophy at Cornell, then composition at
Juilliard and at Mills College in California under Luciano
Berio and Darius Milhaud. The breakthrough came in San
Francisco in 1965. He put two copies of the same tape loop
of a street preacher on two machines. They started in
unison, but the motors ran at slightly different speeds, so
one loop slowly crept ahead of the other. The result, It's
Gonna Rain, and Come Out (1966) were the first phase
pieces.
Back in New York he asked whether people could do what
the tape machines did. Piano Phase (1967) has two pianists
play the same 12-note pattern; one holds tempo while the
other speeds up very slightly until they are one note apart,
then locks in again. Drumming (1970–71) followed a
summer studying with Ewe master drummers in Ghana, and
later study of Balinese gamelan fed the interlocking parts in
his ensemble writing.

From process to orchestra
Music for 18 Musicians (1974–76) was the breakout: a
cycle of eleven chords, pulsing mallets, and phrases
measured by the players' breath. Success brought
commissions from big institutions, and the pieces tonight
trace that move from his own touring group into the
symphony orchestra. From the 1980s he added texts and
subjects: Tehillim (1981), The Desert Music (1982–84) and
Different Trains (1988), which won a Grammy and built its
melodies from recorded speech. Electric Counterpoint
(1987), for Pat Metheny, was later sampled by The Orb.
Double Sextet won the Pulitzer Prize in 2009.

Why electronic musicians claim him
Reich treated tape machines, loops and layering as
compositional tools a decade before the sampler. On "Little
Fluffy Clouds" The Orb sampled Electric Counterpoint,
and the 1999 album Reich Remixed had Coldcut, Howie B,
Ken Ishii and others rework his pieces. Detroit and Berlin
techno share his faith in a fixed grid where change happens
slowly and inside the loop. Autechre's self-mutating
patterns are a distant cousin of his processes, with one big
difference: Reich's process is audible and predictable, and
the drama comes from hearing it happen.

From bombsite to Brutalism
The Cripplegate area, just north of the old Roman and
medieval wall (the "barbican" was a watchtower on that
wall), was flattened in the Blitz on 29 December 1940. The
City of London Corporation chose to rebuild it as housing
rather than offices, and hired architects Chamberlin, Powell
and Bon. The residential estate, with its three towers,
terrace blocks, highwalks and lakes, went up from the
1960s to the mid-1970s. The arts centre came last and was
opened by Queen Elizabeth II on 3 March 1982, who called
it "one of the wonders of the modern world". The whole
complex was listed Grade II in 2001.
It is a famously difficult building to navigate (yellow lines
on the floor exist for a reason) and famously loved: rough
"bush-hammered" concrete, a tropical conservatory on the
roof of the fly tower, and the lakeside terrace you might
have walked past at the interval.

The Hall
The Barbican Hall seats just under 2,000. The original
acoustic was criticised as dry, and the hall has been
reworked more than once, most thoroughly in 2001, when a
new stage canopy and adjustable reflectors went in. The
result is clear and close rather than lush and reverberant,
which suits music like Reich's, where you need to hear
every interlocking note of five pianos and seven
percussionists at once.

Phasing, construction and the 12-pulse grid
Almost everything you heard tonight grows from a handful of rhythmic techniques. Once you can spot
them, the music stops sounding "repetitive" and starts sounding like something is always on the move.

1 · Gradual phasing (the tape-machine idea)
Two identical loops run at very slightly different speeds. At first you hear one fat sound, then a flam, then an echo, then a
brand new rhythm made by the two copies interlocking, until they eventually come back into unison. Reich calls these byproducts resulting patterns: rhythms nobody is playing, which your ear builds from the combination.

2 · Stepped phasing (Clapping Music, Pieces of Wood)
By 1972 Reich replaced the gradual drift with sudden jumps. In Clapping Music both players clap the same 12-pulse pattern;
after a number of repeats, player 2 jumps ahead by one pulse. After twelve jumps they are back in unison. It is the same idea
as rotating a step pattern on a sequencer. Music for Pieces of Wood uses this exact pattern as its root figure.
Each row is one section. Player 1 never changes. Player 2 rotates one step left per section.

3 · Why twelve?
Twelve pulses divide into 2 × 6, 3 × 4, 4 × 3 and 6
× 2. Reich's patterns, like the West African bell
patterns he studied, sit across those groupings, so
your ear keeps switching between hearing them in
threes and in fours. Phasing exploits this: each new
position pushes a different grouping to the front.
That is why the music feels like it's changing when
no note has changed.

4 · Augmentation and breath
Reich stretches note values within a pattern
(augmentation) and measures some phrases by the
length of a breath instead of by bars, so the same
material can feel urgent or spacious. Both appear
tonight.

Riffs, arches and five pairs of claves
Music for a Large Ensemble (1978)
17 minutes · commissioned by the Holland Festival · first performed 1979 by
the Netherlands Wind Ensemble with Steve Reich and Musicians · about 30
players, several amplified
Written right after Music for 18 Musicians and the biggest group Reich
had used so far. The engine is a carpet of short, repeating riffs on pianos,
marimbas and xylophones, with strings and winds layered on top.
The process is melodic expansion and contraction. Each of the four
sections is an arch, A B C B A: short riffs get longer step by step, reach a
peak, and shrink back. At each of the four peaks, four trumpets play
broad sustained chords, each written "to take one comfortable breath to
perform". So the long notes are timed by human lungs, not by bar lines,
and they float over a machine-tight pulse.

Music for Pieces of Wood (1973)
10 minutes · five pairs of tuned claves · tonight: Neil Percy, David Jackson,
Sam Walton, Patrick King, Aidy Spillett
A touring piece: Reich wanted music he could play anywhere without "a
vanload of gear". Each player has a pair of hardwood claves tuned to a
different pitch. The root figure is the Clapping Music pattern.
The process is rhythmic construction. One player holds a steady pulse
(a human metronome) and another plays the root figure. The others then
build the same figure one beat at a time, replacing rests with hits until the
pattern is complete. Each player enters at a different offset, so when the
pattern is complete it is phased against the others and pushes a different
accent to the front.
The arc: the root figure shrinks from 6 beats to 4 to 3 across the three
parts, so the cycles get tighter and the texture denser. The players
crescendo as it fills up. Reich called it "one of the loudest I have ever
composed". With five pitches sounding together, the unison fortissimo
near the end is close to a drum machine with every voice on.

Reich's take on the chaconne, the Baroque form where a repeating chord
sequence carries a set of variations (Pachelbel's Canon is the famous
one). Here the chord cycle is stretched to an epic scale: three passes of
about six, ten and nine minutes. Each chord can last a long time.
On top, winds and keyboards play a constant stream of melody in canon,
the same line entering at different points so it overlaps itself. That's
phasing written out. The canons get more florid with each pass. Strings
and brass hold the long chords underneath.
Rhythm to listen for: two clocks at once. The surface moves in quick
even pulses, the harmony moves like weather. This is the same split as a
techno track with a 16th-note sequence over a pad that changes every 16
bars, and it shows how long you can hold a chord if the surface keeps
moving. Max Richter, whose On the Nature of Daylight uses the same
chaconne idea, names Reich as a major influence.

Three Movements (1986)
15 minutes · written for the St Louis Symphony and Leonard Slatkin · full
orchestra in three linked movements, fast–slow–fast
Built on the same slow chord changes as the Variations, but darker and
stormier. Reich wrote that the chord changes "may suggest the changing
light as clouds move slowly across the sky".
Harmony: lots of diminished seventh chords, which contain the tritone
and can resolve in several directions, so the key never feels settled. It
ends on A minor, but with an added seventh ringing that keeps it open.

The layout is part of the rhythm. Reich asks for the strings to be split
into two equal groups on either side of a central "engine room" of
marimbas, vibraphones and pianos. The keyboard players at the centre
carry the pulse and everyone can see them. Reich calls it "one unified
rhythm direction".
The finale uses a mensuration canon: the same melody played at two
speeds at once, one copy three times slower than the other. Sibelius used
the same trick at the end of his Fifth Symphony. In sequencer terms, it's
one track's pattern copied to another track with a clock divider of 3.

Music for Ensemble and Orchestra (2018)
20 minutes · five continuous movements · first performed by the Los Angeles Philharmonic under Gustavo Dudamel at Walt Disney
Concert Hall, November 2018 · the LSO gave the UK premiere the same year

Reich's first orchestral piece in about 30 years, and the one that pulls together everything else on tonight's
programme.
A modern concerto grosso. In the Baroque concerto
grosso a small group of soloists trades material with the full
orchestra. Reich's soloist group is 20 players: strings,
winds, brass, electric bass, vibraphones and pianos.
Because there are so many solo parts, the music feels like
chamber music, which lets Reich, as he says, "write the
intricate, detailed music that I like to write". Soloists step
out of the texture with short bursts of melody that set off
chain reactions in the rest of the group "like cells dividing".

A new kind of pattern. In the second movement Reich
writes ostinatos made from slowly rising scales, something
he had never done before ("but they work!"). He calls it a
tip of the hat to the opening of Bach's Fifth Brandenburg
Concerto, which is itself a kind of concerto grosso.
The big shape is an arch of pulse speed. The piano vamp
drives all five movements, but its basic note value changes
from movement to movement. The texture slows from 16th
notes to 8ths to quarter notes in the centre, then speeds back
up. It's the same ABCBA arch as Music for a Large
Ensemble, applied to tempo feel instead of riff length. The
real tempo doesn't change; only the density of the pulse
does.

Sound world. The ostinatos are more angular than in his
earlier pieces, built from unusual scales and doubled in
octaves or large unisons. The electric bass with piano and
vibraphone gives a sound halfway between jazz and
gamelan. The harmony floats, often without a clear home
key, like Three Movements.

8 · TA K I N G I T H O M E

Reich ideas on your rig
Five techniques from tonight, set up on your modular, VCV Rack and Live. The last two also help with
arrangement: Reich's arches are a way to move a track somewhere without breaking the loop.

1 · Clapping Music on Pam's New Workout
Set two Pam's outputs to Euclidean with 12 steps and 7 hits,
the West African bell pattern behind Drumming. Send both
to two short, different voices (Zadar envelopes into
Mangrove, or two Freak hits) panned apart. Leave output 1
alone. Every 8 bars, turn output 2's rotation one step on.
After 12 moves you are back in unison. To automate it, put
a slow stepped CV on output 2's rotation from KAccumulator or a DROID clock divider.

2 · True phasing: one clock drifts
Clock one Metropolix track from Live via the disting NT as
usual. Run a second voice from Marbles' internal clock
(unpatch its clock input), set as close to the master as you
can, with the same 12-step pattern. The tiny tempo
difference does the tape-machine trick: unison, flam, echo,
new pattern. In VCV, use two clock modules at 120 and
120.3 BPM driving the same sequence.

3 · Rhythmic construction on Metropolix
Copy your root pattern to a second track with every stage
silenced (gate mode off). Switch one stage back on every 4
or 8 bars until it matches, then do it again on a third track
starting at a different stage. Each new entry builds the
pattern at a different offset, as in Pieces of Wood. Then
shorten the track length from 12 to 8 to 6 to tighten the
cycle, as the claves go from 6 beats to 4 to 3.

4 · Mensuration canon
Put the same melody on two Metropolix tracks and set the
second track's clock divider to 3 (or 2 for something
busier). Send the slow copy to Three Sisters or Just Friends
for a pad-like line that shadows the lead. It sounds simple
but the two copies never line up the same way twice.

5 · Arch form for arrangement
Instead of adding and removing parts, change the pulse
density of one driving part across the track: 16ths → 8ths
→ quarters → 8ths → 16ths, as in Music for Ensemble and
Orchestra. Clock-divide your main vamp by 1, 2, 4 per
section from DROID or Long Form, with the drums staying
at full speed. Then steal the Variations trick: take a 4- or 6chord Hapax progression and give each chord 8 to 16 bars,
so the harmony becomes the slow arc and the loops sit on
top. For a breath-length swell, use a Just Friends or Cold
Mac envelope triggered by hand rather than by the clock.

Start here: idea 1 takes ten minutes and only needs Pam's and two voices. Record 12 rotations into Live, pick the four
best positions, and you have the drum skeleton for an arrangement that develops without new parts.
