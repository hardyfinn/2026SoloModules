# Acid house 1987 to 1992 musical analysis

## Summary
A musical analysis of classic Chicago acid house built on the TB-303, 1987 to 1992.
The full 16-page PDF text is included.

## Question
> New thread, a PDF on Acid House. I'm interested in an analysis of classic Acid House, such as the works of Armando, Mike Dunn, Lil Louis. This really is limited to from 1987 through to 1992, shall we say. Chicago artists in the main. By Acid House, I do mean works using a Roland TB-303. It's as simple as that. Often paired with a 707 or a 909. The only other artist outside of that scene would be a guy called Gerald. What I'm interested in is anything in terms of a musical analysis. See what you can find.

## Answer
The Acid House PDF is ready: Acid-House-1987-1992-Analysis.pdf, 16 pages.

The main finding is that nobody has ever reliably published step-by-step transcriptions, tempos or keys for these records. What survives is interviews and a few magazine articles from the time. So every claim in the guide is marked as one of three things: **sourced** (with a reference), **inferred** (my reasoning from the sources), or **illustrative** (example patterns I wrote to teach a technique, not copied from a record).

**What I did find:**
- **"Acid Tracks":** it was made in one knob-twisting session, and the tempo was slowed from about 128 to about 120 BPM on Marshall Jefferson's advice. A 1989 Music Technology article describes the bassline as a five-note riff that pulls against the four-four beat for 11 minutes, and only the sound changes. The drums were a 707 and a 727.
- **Armando's "Land of Confusion":** Mike Dunn says the bassline is the pattern that came up after taking the 303's batteries out and putting them back. Paul Johnson says Armando secretly ran two 303s at once.
- **"Voodoo Ray":** Gerald's 808 was his master clock, driving two SH-101s and the 303. The sources disagree on which synth plays the bass. Sound on Sound quotes Gerald saying the 303 counter-riff "covered up the timing".
- **"French Kiss":** none of the sources I found says it uses a 303. I kept it as the borderline case because its slowdown to a near-stop and back is a strong answer to your arrangement problem.
- **Gaps:** I found no production detail at all for Dunn's "Magic Feet" or "Phantasy Girl", or for Bam Bam's "Where's Your Child?".

The guide also covers what makes acid work musically, with recipes for Ableton, Metropolix plus the disting NT, and VCV. There's a section on using these ideas at Detroit tempos and in the Autechre direction, and a listening worksheet with a blank 303 grid so you can measure tempo, key and pattern from the records yourself.

What a TB-303 actually does
Acid is the sound of people using a bass machine's quirks as the composition. You can't analyse the records
without knowing what the machine can and can't do, because every "decision" on these tracks is shaped by it.

The voice
One oscillator (saw or square, a switch), into a resonant lowpass filter, into a VCA. Five knobs shape it live: Cutoff,
Resonance, Env Mod (how far the envelope opens the
filter), Decay (how long the filter envelope lasts) and Accent
(how hard accented steps hit). Music Technology in 1989 lists
exactly these as the four knobs "Marshall Jefferson and DJ
Pierre" turned in real time on "Acid Tracks", and calls the
result "timbral modulation". SOURCED [MT89a]
The same article is blunt that the 303's resonance on its own
is "weak", and guesses that "Acid Tracks" went through some
extra external filter. That is the writer's speculation, not
established. SOURCED as speculation

The battery trick
Mike Dunn says the "Land of Confusion" bassline was a
pattern that came up after pulling the batteries and putting
them back. SOURCED [5M] The 303 has no factory pattern
bank in ROM, so what appears after power loss is most likely
scrambled memory: in effect a random pattern. INFERRED
Either way, the lesson is the same: a lot of classic acid is a
found pattern, kept because it worked on a dancefloor, not
composed.

The drum machines
MACHINE ROLE ON THESE RECORDS

TR-707

The cheap workhorse: kick, clap, hats. Roland's
own write-up of Pierre's Phuture kit names the
"bread-and-butter TR-707 and Latin-infused TR727". [Roland22]

An accented step is louder, and it shortens the filter decay and
pushes the filter harder. With resonance up, consecutive
accents stack up a little extra filter sweep, which is the "wow"
that makes repeated accented notes squeal and climb. This is
why accent placement is as much a rhythm as the notes
themselves. HARDWARE BEHAVIOUR

TR-727

Same engine, Latin percussion (congas, bongos,
timbales, cuíca, agogô). The swinging layer over
the 707.

TR-808

"I've Lost Control" was "just an 808 and a TB303" (Jefferson). Gerald used it as his master
sequencer. [WHD, SOS15]

Slide

TR-909

Arrives later in the period. Jefferson said in 1989
he had only recently started using one. [MT89b]

Accent

A slide step holds the gate open into the next note and glides
pitch over a short fixed time (roughly 60 ms). No envelope
retrigger, so a slid pair sounds like one rubbery note that
bends. Slides into an octave jump are the classic "yowl".

Paul Johnson recalls the Armando/Dunn circle pooling "an
808, a 707, a 909, a 626, a 727, and then we'd have two
303s". [5M]

The sequencer
Pitches and timing are entered separately: first the notes, then
the rhythm (note, tie or rest per step). Patterns are up to 16
sixteenth-steps (or 12 triplet eighths), one octave on the
keypad plus octave up/down buttons. SOURCED [MT89a]
Because pitch and time are separate lists, editing one shifts
how the other lines up, which is the source of a lot of "happy
accident" patterns. The 303 has no CV/gate input, only DIN
sync, so in 1987 it was slaved to a Roland drum machine or
played alone.

Sleezy D, "I've Lost Control"

Trax TX 113 · finished 1984–85, released 1986 · produced by Marshall Jefferson · vocal Derrick "Sleezy D" Harris
This is where the 303 enters house, and it is instructive
because it is not yet acid. Jefferson could not sync his TR808 to his Yamaha QX1 sequencer, went looking for
something that would sync to the 808, and landed on the TB303. SOURCED [MT89b] His own description: "That song, it
was just an 808 and a TB-303." [WHD]
Music Technology in 1989 notes that the 303 "is used in a
restrained way on the track", with none of the timbral
movement that later defined acid. [MT89b] Jefferson said he
"wanted to basically put a nervous breakdown on vinyl"; the
menace is in the vocal and the drum machine, with the 303 as
a bass instrument. It sold around 70,000 copies in Chicago.
[MT89b]
Then the key handover: "I gave Pierre my TB303 because I
was just tired of using it, I didn't like it any more." [MT89b]
(Pierre's own accounts say Spanky bought a used 303 for
around $40. Both may be true at different times; the sources
don't reconcile it.)

What to take from it
Sync drove the instrument choice. The 303 got into
house because it was the box that would lock to the drum
machine. That is the same problem you have with an ad
hoc MIDI rig.
Static timbre = bass instrument; moving timbre = lead
instrument. Same machine, two different jobs. The jump
from this record to "Acid Tracks" is entirely about the
knobs moving.
A side technique from Jefferson's studio (used on "Move
Your Body", not a 303 record): play a part at about 40
BPM and speed the sequencer up to 122, lengthening
notes so they sound full at speed. [RBMA16] Useful for
Metropolix step entry of fast lines.

Phuture, "Acid Tracks"

Trax, 1987 · DJ Pierre (Nathan Jones), Earl "Spanky" Smith Jr, Herbert "Herb J" Jackson · mixed with Marshall Jefferson · originally
titled "In Your Mind"

The pattern

How it was made
Spanky had "a beat plan" but none of them knew how to
program the 303. Pierre "started just tweaking knobs and
turning stuff", Spanky said "keep doing that", and in that first
session, "an hour or two, just twisting knobs and
programming things", they made the track. SOURCED
[RBMA12] Pierre to The Fader: "We said forget trying to make a
baseline, let's program it like this and just twist the knobs."
[Fader14] Spanky dropped drums in and out while Pierre
worked the 303: "like a jam session and we recorded the
whole thing." [RBMA12] Recorded to cassette; Ron Hardy
played it four times at the Music Box before the crowd went
for it. [RBMA12, Fader14]
Jefferson's role, in Pierre's words: "setting levels and stuff.
But as far as producing, he didn't add any new sounds to it."
[RBMA12]

Tempo
Pierre: it was "going like 128" in Spanky's room and
Jefferson suggested slowing it to 120. [RBMA12] Wikipedia
gives c. 130 down to about 120; Roland's 2022 article says
119. [WKAT, Roland22] Working figure: about 120 BPM, and
the slowdown was a deliberate production decision.

Music Technology (Feb 1989): "a syncopated five-note riff
repeated (with occasional variations) on the 303 over a house
beat for 11 minutes and 17 seconds", a "syncopated crossrhythm" that "tugs against the 4/4 beat". SOURCED [MT89a]
Davis's 2022 paper on acid notation also describes it as one
short pattern repeated for about 12 minutes, with pitch fixed
and only the timbre changing. [CAM22]
No reliable step transcription is published. The grid below is
an illustrative five-note cross-rhythm cell of the kind
described: notes three steps apart so the figure cycles against
the beat. Check the real record and rewrite the notes to match.

The drums and mix
Roland's write-up of Pierre's own kit: TR-707 plus TR-727,
with "compression, reverb, mixing, and seriously aggressive
panning" all part of the character, and "nothing in Pierre's
bank is ever panned center". [Roland22] That is a modern
preset bank, so read it as Pierre's taste rather than proof of the
1987 mix.

Structure
Length 11:17 per Music Technology, 12:16 on the Trax 12inch listing. [MT89a, WKAT] One pattern, no chord changes, no
melody beyond the 303: the structure is drums in and out
(Spanky's hands) plus the filter journey (Pierre's hands).

5 · 1987–1992

Armando and Mike Dunn: Warehouse acid
Armando Gallop was 17 when he made "Land of Confusion" (Westbrook, 1987). He died in December 1996,
aged 26. Mike Dunn was his engineer, remixer and label partner (Warehouse, Muzique, Dance Mutha), and says
"I was the one recording" Armando's material. [HOC, WKA, 5M]

How they recorded

"Land of Confusion" (1987)
Dunn says the bassline was "a pre-set bassline": pull the 303's
batteries, put them back, and it was the first pattern that came
up. SOURCED [5M, HOC] The track started life on a tape Dunn
forgot to erase, originally titled "Slave for Love". [5M] Pierre
calls it "one funky acid line" that "you moved your body
different" to, and says Armando "was also a master at using a
303". [RBMA12] Kevin Starke: "I've never heard anyone put out
an acid bassline like Armando did." [909o, 5M]

Dunn: "All we did was put the music on the reel-to-reel. We
had the drums synced up so the drums would always play
live." Masters went to cassette and the reels were erased, so
the multitracks are gone. [5M] The arrangement was therefore
performed: drum machine running live, 303 knobs and mutes
played during the pass. INFERRED

"151" and "Downfall" (1988)

Versions on the Westbrook 12-inch: Confusion Mix 7:28,
Confusion Track 1 4:14, Track 2 4:25. The 1988 Warehouse
remix EP (WH-001) opens with Dunn's "Dunn 4 Funn Mixx"
(6:41). [HOC]

"151 (Original Version)" 4:56 is on the 1988 Warehouse EP;
DJ Pap calls it "the second coming of acid music". Djax-UpBeats reissued it with remixes in 1992 (Speedy J, Terrace).
"Downfall" (Trax Acid Trax, 1988, 4:50). [HOC, 5M] No
technical detail survives for either beyond the 303.

Two 303s at once

Mike Dunn

Paul Johnson: "He never would tell us how he got the 303 to
run two different sequences at once", then later, "he had two
303s and he never told us!" [5M] So a signature of Armando's
sound is two interlocking acid lines, synced from the same
drum machine. SOURCED (one witness)

Dunn calls himself "the 808 man": "I used both the 303 and
808", and of the 303 today, "I'm just messing with stuff onthe-fly." [XLR8R18, MR18] He learned editing by "pause editing"
on cassette and chopping reel-to-reel. [MR18, MEOKO16] His
first release was "Dance You Mutha" (Westbrook, 1987).
"Magic Feet" and "Phantasy Girl": I found no source with
any production detail on either, only title mentions. On craft,
his rule is melodic: the track works "if the melody sticks in a
persons head". [MEOKO16]

The one documented harmony move: Tyree's piano
Nearly every record above is harmonically static: one pattern, one root, no chords. "Acid Over" is the exception Pierre
points to, a jazz piano laid over the acid. That is the closest the canon comes to your Hapax chord-playground workflow.
The trick that makes it work ( INFERRED ): the 303 stays on its pedal root and the piano moves above it, so the chords
colour the line rather than forcing it to follow changes. Extended chords (minor 9ths, 11ths) sit well because the 303
already implies the minor third and flat seventh.

Lil Louis, "French Kiss"

Diamond Records (a Warehouse subsidiary) 1989, then FFRR / Epic · 9:52 · UK No. 2, US Dance No. 1 · banned by the BBC
Is it a 303 record? None of the six sources I read
(Wikipedia, Resident Advisor's Rewind, DJ Mag, two
analysis blogs) says it uses a TB-303, and none names the
instrument on the main riff: one calls it an organ riff, one a
five-note riff with a high string, one a "synth power chord
motif". One blog says DX100 patches make the chords and
melodies. SOURCED [WKFK, RA20, HHE] By your definition it
is probably not acid house. It is here because of what it does
to an arrangement.

Pitch
Wikipedia: "based on a single note (F-natural)". Resident
Advisor: a repeating five-note riff, "transposed down and up
again midway through". One blog's tags say F♯ major, which
conflicts. [WKFK, RA20, HHE] Treat F as the working root and
check it.

Tempo: the whole point
The track runs in the 120–125 BPM range, then slows,
apparently to a near-stop around 45 BPM, holds there through
the moaning section, then speeds back up for a final two
minutes. [HHE, RA20, WKFK] The 45 BPM figure is from one
blog and is unverified. Armand Van Helden: "the first dance
track I've known to date to change bpms drastically". [WKFK]
Mike Golding on hearing it at a rave: "When it started
slowing down I genuinely thought the rave had been busted".
[RA20]

Why it matters to you
It answers your arrangement problem directly. The loop never
changes; the clock is the arrangement. Tempo becomes a
fader. Derrick May "played it relentlessly for decades", often
mixing in while it sped back up. [RA20] Your Pivot module
(metric modulation, LAND back on the bar line) is a tool
built for this kind of move.

8 · MANCHESTER, 1988

A Guy Called Gerald, "Voodoo Ray"

Rham! Records, 1988 · Moonraker Studios, Manchester, summer 1988 · UK No. 12 in 1989, 18 weeks in the chart · first 500 pressed
sold out in a day

The vocal

The rig
Gerald's TR-808 was "my main sequencer". Two of its trigger
outputs clocked two Roland SH-101s (one from the cowbell,
one from the accent output, by his account), and the 808's
DIN sync drove the TB-303. No MIDI. SOURCED
[SOS15, MR24g] He found the trigger-to-clock trick by trial and
error, without a manual. [RBMA13]

Which synth plays what
The sources disagree. Music Technology (1990) says the SH101 played the "Voodoo Ray" bassline, with a "steel-drum"
quality, and the 303 played the acid on the EP's other three
tracks. [MT90] Sound on Sound (2015) has Gerald saying "I
got the 303 to do a counter riff and I think it kind of covered
up the timing". [SOS15] Both can be true: SH-101s carry the
bass and a 303 adds a counter-line. Red Bull's feature says
layered bass parts with different reverbs produced the
metallic steel-drum effect. [RBMA17]

Timing as a feature
Getting the second SH-101 sequence to line up was hit and
miss: "I had an issue with timing." [SOS15] The 303 counterriff was partly there to mask it. In the studio a SMPTE-toDIN box locked the rig to the 16-track. [SOS15] The loose lock
between sequencers is part of why the record breathes.

Accounts differ on who sang. All agree a vocal phrase was
sampled into an Akai sampler and reversed: "It was basically
the sound reversed back on itself." [SOS15] "Voodoo ray" is
Peter Cook saying "voodoo rage" on a Derek & Clive record,
cut short in the sampler. [SOS15, RBMA17]

How he thinks
"I was trying to mimic a polyphonic synthesizer using a
monophonic synthesizer." "It was all synthesis and drum
machines and no loops." [SOS15] On the 303: "I was
programming it like the drum machine so I didn't realise that
it was supposed to be hard." He worked out the melody first,
then tapped in the rhythm. [MR24g] On arranging: "I knew
nothing about counting bars." It was mixed live, several
people muting and unmuting; six versions in one session.
[RBMA17]

Effects on separate outs
The 808's separate outputs let each drum get its own
treatment: a snare EQ'd for snap, a second lower snare made
by pitching the tape, flanger on hats, reverb and chorus on
bass, guitar pedals on drum outs. [SOS15, MR24g]

9 · SYNTHESIS

What makes acid work: the musical grammar
Putting the sourced accounts together with how the 303 behaves, these are the principles that recur across the
records. Each is INFERRED from the evidence in Sections 2–8 unless marked.

1 · The pattern is fixed, the timbre is the
melody
"Acid Tracks" holds one pattern for 11+ minutes; pitch never
changes, timbre does. [MT89a, CAM22] Cutoff, resonance, env
mod and decay are the "notes" of the arrangement. Think of
them as four slow melodies played over one bar.

2 · Cross-rhythm against the four-four
The riff "tugs against the 4/4 beat". [MT89a] Groupings of 3
(or 5, or 7) steps inside 16 make the pattern land differently
against the kick each beat. Accents placed on that grouping,
not on the kick, make two rhythms audible from one line.

3 · Accents and slides are a second and third
rhythm
Notes, accents and slides are separate lanes. When the three
lanes have different patterns (or, on a modern sequencer,
different lengths), the line seems to change constantly while
staying the same.

4 · Pitch: a root pedal plus colour notes

5 · Drums as mute performance
Spanky "dropping beats in and out" while Pierre turned
knobs; Dunn's drums "always play live" to reel-to-reel;
Gerald mixing "live, in real time". [RBMA12, 5M, RBMA17] The
arrangement was a two-person live performance captured in
one pass.

6 · Simple drums, panned wide
707 kick on every beat, clap on 2 and 4, off-beat hats, and the
727 percussion carrying the swing, with things panned off
centre. [Roland22]

7 · The found pattern is respected
Battery-reset patterns, knob accidents, a dropped "rage". The
craft is recognising a good accident and committing to it for
ten minutes.

8 · Length
Original mixes run 7–12 minutes. They are built for DJs, so
the slow evolution is the point. Loop fatigue is avoided not by
changing the loop but by changing what you hear in it.

The 303 keypad covers one octave plus octave jumps, so
lines live on one root with the octave, flat third, fourth, fifth
and flat seventh as colour. That points to minor-pentatonic /
Aeolian / Phrygian territory: the flat second (a semitone
above the root) is the darkest, most "acid" colour. Octave
jumps with a slide are the yelp.

Doing it in Ableton, VCV Rack and the rig
Visual-patching recipes, no code. Each gives the signal flow, starting values and a couple of variations.

A · Ableton Live only
1. Drum Rack with 707/727 samples (or your own
707 and 727 in VCV). Kick on every beat, clap 2 and 4,
hat on the off-beats; set the track's groove to a 16th swing
around 56%.
2. Bass: Analog (or Operator) with one oscillator, saw, lowpass 24 dB, resonance about 70%, filter envelope amount
high, decay 150–300 ms, no sustain. Glide in Legato
mode with a short time (about 60 ms) gives 303-style
slides: overlap two notes in the clip to slide.
3. Accent: map MIDI velocity to both amp and filter
envelope amount. High velocity = accent.
4. Draw a one-bar clip and loop it for the whole track. Make
the arrangement only with clip automation on cutoff,
resonance, env amount and decay, using long, wave-like
curves over 8–32 bars (Figure 3).
5. Variations: put an overdrive (Roar or Saturator) after the
filter for later-period acid; or add a second clip of a
different length (12 or 14 steps) on another instance for
the Armando two-machine interlock.

B · Metropolix + disting NT (+ Pam's)
Metropolix has per-stage accent and slide built in, which
makes it the most 303-like sequencer you own.
1. Clock: Live master clock through the NT (CV Tools), into
Pam's; Pam's x4 (16ths) into Metropolix clock.
2. Voice: Metropolix pitch into an oscillator (a saw from the
NT or any VCO), then into a resonant filter: Vult Freak or
Three Sisters (low-pass out, quality high). Metropolix gate
into Zadar (fast attack, decay ~200 ms) feeding filter CV
and a VCA.

4. Slide: Metropolix slide per stage, glide time short.
5. The knob journey: a slow Pam's LFO (or a Zadar longloop shape, 32–64 bars) into filter cutoff CV; play
resonance by hand.
6. Cross-rhythm: set Metropolix to 12 or 14 stages against
the 16-step kick, or give the accent lane its own length.
Variation: a Marbles T-section gate with low jitter into the
Zadar accent input gives random accents on top of the fixed
line. Or DROID logic: accent only when two clock divisions
coincide (e.g. /3 AND /4), which makes accents fall every 12
steps.

C · VCV Rack
1. Clock (your Live link or Pivot) into a step sequencer with
per-step gate/accent/slide (any sequencer with per-step
accent and slide; Kleener is good for found bit patterns).
2. VCO (saw) into a resonant low-pass filter, VCA, with two
ADSRs, one on gate and one on accent, both summed into
cutoff CV. A slew limiter on pitch, switched on only for
slide steps, gives the glide.
3. A slow LFO, or your Krka / Lilt for long non-repeating
curves, on cutoff and env amount.
4. Variation: duplicate the voice with a 12-step sequence for
two interlocking lines (Armando); or put Pivot on the
master clock and make a "French Kiss" slowdown and
LAND back on Live's bar.

D · The found-pattern habit
Copy the battery trick: randomise a pattern (Marbles into
Metropolix record, Kleener, or Live 12's MIDI Generators
such as Seed or Rhythm), loop it for 2 minutes, and keep it
only if it still grooves. Then fix it and only touch the knobs.

3. Accent: A Metropolix aux output set to accent, into a
second Zadar channel (shorter, sharper) summed into the
filter CV and the VCA. Two envelopes = normal and
accent.

Acid for Detroit / Autechre ears
Your two stated problems are loop fatigue and not being able to steer a track somewhere. Acid house solved
exactly that in 1987 with a rule: never change the loop, change the listener's attention. Here is how to carry the
idea up-tempo and into self-mutating territory.

1 · Timbre as arrangement, at techno tempo
At 128–135 BPM, one 16-step 303 pattern can carry eight
minutes if cutoff, resonance and env mod move in differentlength cycles, e.g. 24, 40 and 56 bars. Those cycles realign
only rarely, so the line keeps sounding new, which is a gentle,
controllable version of Autechre's mutation.

5 · Tempo as a section marker
Borrow "French Kiss": one controlled tempo dip (say 132
down to 100 over 16 bars and back) as the breakdown,
instead of removing the drums. Pivot can do this and land
back on Live's bar line.

3 · Two machines, Armando style
A low, busy root line plus a high, sparse counter-line
(Gerald's "polyphonic from monophonic"). Over Hapax

below.

Where Chicago changed things by hand, use logic: DROID or
VCV gates that, every 32 bars, transpose one step, flip one
slide, or move one accent. Small, rule-driven mutation keeps
the "same track" identity.

Listening worksheet
Use this on any record in Section 6 to fill the gaps the sources leave. About ten minutes per track.
1. Tempo. Drop the file in Live, turn Warp on, and read the
detected BPM, then confirm by tapping. Old vinyl rips are
often ±2%; trust the tap.
2. Root. Loop a bar where the 303 is most open. Put a Tuner
on the track, or play a sine bass under it in Live until it
locks. Write the root and whether the 303 sits sharp or flat.
3. Step count. Count how many 16ths before the 303 repeats
exactly: 16, or a shorter loop (12, 8, 6) cycling against the
bar.
4. Notes. Slow the loop to 50% with Complex Pro warping
(keeps pitch) and hum or play each note onto the grid
below.
5. Accents. Mark the loud, sharp, brighter notes. They often
follow a different rhythm from the kick.
