### Full primer

### Bulgarian a cappella groups: a primer for producers

Written from general knowledge, not fresh web research. Dates and names are the well-documented ones; worth double-checking before quoting anywhere.

#### The groups worth knowing

**Le Mystère des Voix Bulgares** (Bulgarian State Radio & Television Female Vocal Choir)
The one most people in the West know. Swiss producer Marcel Cellier compiled the choir's radio recordings on his own label in 1975. Ivo Watts-Russell reissued it on 4AD in 1986, where it became a cult record with the Cocteau Twins / Dead Can Dance crowd. Volume 2 won a Grammy in 1990. "Le Mystère des Voix Bulgares" later became the choir's working name. In 2018 they released *BooCheeMish* with Lisa Gerrard.

**Philip Koutev Ensemble** (founded 1951)
Koutev is the composer who invented the style. He took village songs (traditionally sung solo or by two or three women) and arranged them for a large choir, keeping the drones and dissonant intervals but adding Western ideas about harmony and form. Almost every famous "Bulgarian voices" piece comes from Koutev or his followers (Krasimir Kyurkchiyski, Petar Lyondev, Nikolai Kaufman).

**Trio Bulgarka** (Yanka Rupkina, Stoyanka Boneva, Eva Georgieva)
Three soloists from different regions. Best known outside Bulgaria for singing on Kate Bush's *The Sensual World* (1989) and *The Red Shoes* (1993). A good way to hear the sound inside a pop/production context.

**Angelite** (Bulgarian Voices Angelite)
Choir that went furthest into crossovers: *Fly, Fly My Sadness* (1996) with Tuvan throat singers Huun-Huur-Tu and the Moscow Art Trio. Drone + overtone singing + seconds is a very sound-design-friendly combination.

**Eva Quartet**
Small group of singers from the Mystère choir. Closer to the village scale of the music, so the individual voices and the dissonance are much clearer than in the big choir.

Also: Valya Balkanska's Rhodope song *Izlel e Delyo Haydutin* (solo voice with kaba gaida bagpipe) is on the Voyager Golden Record. Not a cappella, but the same vocal world.

#### What makes the sound

1. **Seconds, not thirds.** Village two-part singing (especially from the Shop region around Sofia) puts the two voices a major or minor second apart and holds them there. Western ears hear it as a clash; here it is the point. The rub creates a beating, buzzing quality, almost like two detuned oscillators.
2. **Drone.** One voice (or section) holds a single note, often the tonic, while the melody moves around it in small steps. The melody keeps returning to land a second away from the drone.
3. **Open-throat, forward voice.** Chest voice pushed high, bright and nasal, very little vibrato. Lots of upper harmonics, which is why it cuts through like a synth lead.
4. **Ornaments.** *Izvikvane* (a whoop or yelp at the end of a phrase, a glottal flip up) and fast trills and shakes on held notes.
5. **Aksak (limping) meters.** Dance songs use uneven groups of 2s and 3s:
   - Rachenitsa: 7/8 (2+2+3)
   - Daychovo: 9/8 (2+2+2+3)
   - Kopanitsa: 11/16 (2+2+3+2+2)
   - Paydushko: 5/16 (2+3)
   Slow songs are often free time, no meter at all.
6. **Phrase endings resolve the tension.** The seconds open up to a unison or octave at a cadence. The music lives on tension and release done with interval width rather than chord changes.

#### Ways in for your music

**Harmony (Hapax chord playground and beyond)**
- Swap thirds for seconds: sus2 and add2 voicings, or literally a two-note cluster (C + D) held as a pad. Stack two of them a fifth apart (C D G A) for the choir thickness.
- Drone plus moving line: hold a root on one voice (Just Friends in a sustained mode, or a disting NT oscillator) and have Metropolix play a stepwise melody in a narrow range around it. Bias the scale so it lands a second above or below the drone on strong beats.
- Arrangement lever: instead of changing chords to move a section forward, change the interval width. Unison/octave = calm, fifth = open, second = tense. A whole build can be "drone stays, interval narrows." This is a direct answer to loop fatigue: the loop stays, the tension changes.

**Sound design (getting the voice without samples)**
- **Mangrove** is a formant oscillator and is the natural fit: two Mangroves (or one plus a pitched VCO) detuned a whole tone apart, bright formant setting, minimal vibrato.
- **Three Sisters** in formant mode over a buzzy saw gives the nasal "ah/eh" vowel. Slowly sweep the formant for vowel shifts.
- Add a short pitch flick up at note ends (envelope into pitch, or a Cold Mac slope) to imitate the izvikvane whoop.

**Rhythm (aksak meets Detroit)**
- Keep the four-on-the-floor kick and put a 7-step pattern (accents on steps 1, 3, 5 = 2+2+3 in 16ths) on hats or percussion. It phases against the 16-step bar and lines up again every 7 bars. Metropolix with track length 7 or a Pam's output with a 7-step Euclidean pattern does this.
- For something more Autechre: alternate 7 and 9 step groupings (rachenitsa and daychovo) under control of a DROID or Marbles decision, so the limp keeps changing.

**Sampling**
- The State choir recordings are copyrighted and actively licensed, so clearing them for an EP release is non-trivial. Options: resynthesise (above), record singers, use cleared sample libraries, or keep samples for sketches and replace them later.
- If you do sample: long held drones and the seconds clusters are the most usable bits. Granular stretching (disting NT has granular algorithms) on a drone section makes great pads.

## Question 2
> I'm really interested in what makes this music so distinctive, with an eye (ear?) to making another vcv rack module that exploits any typical behaviours. Am i right in thinking that there is an element of microtonality to the harmonies which the adept vocalists can utilise?

## Answer 2
Yes, you're right, especially in the village style the choirs grew out of. The famous "seconds" often aren't tempered seconds at all. In Shop-region two-part singing (the Bistritsa grannies are the classic example), field measurements put the interval anywhere from about a quarter-tone up to a whole tone, often around 150 cents, between a minor and a major second. Singers adjust it by ear to get the strongest "ringing" against the drone. The big State choir arrangements drift closer to equal temperament because of their size, but the soloists still bend notes.

The useful point for a module is what they're tuning *for*. It's beating and roughness, not a fixed interval. At their pitch range (around 400 to 700 Hz), a 150-cent gap beats at roughly 30 to 50 Hz, right where roughness is strongest. So the musical target is closer to "a beat rate in Hz" than "a scale degree".

That suggests a module where a drone voice is set by V/oct and the second voice is tuned to a target beat rate rather than an interval, so the rub stays at the same intensity as the pitch moves. You'd get a "tension" knob that sweeps from unison through the roughness zone to an open fifth, plus an izvikvane pitch flick at note ends and an aksak clock (2+2+3 and so on).
